# Senior/Staff Question Bank and Offer Negotiation

## How to use this file

This file has two parts. The first part is a bank of 40 real behavioral questions, grouped by theme, that senior and staff engineers commonly report seeing in FAANG-style loops. For each question, you'll find a short note on what interviewers are actually listening for, so you know how to aim your story bank answer from file 01.

The second part covers something many engineers under-prepare for: what happens after you pass. Negotiating a senior-level offer is a skill of its own, and leaving money or level on the table here can cost you far more than any single interview round.

## Part 1: 40 behavioral questions grouped by theme

### Theme: Leadership (6 questions)

1. **Tell me about a time you led a project without being the official lead.**
   What interviewers listen for:
   - Did you take initiative without waiting for permission or a title?
   - Did you coordinate other people effectively, or just do the work yourself?
   - Was there a measurable outcome from your leadership?

2. **Describe a time you had to set technical direction for a team.**
   - Did you gather input from others, or decide unilaterally?
   - How did you communicate the direction and get buy-in?
   - Did the direction hold up over time, or did you need to course-correct?

3. **Tell me about a time you had to make an unpopular decision.**
   - Did you explain your reasoning clearly, even if people disagreed?
   - Did you show empathy for the people affected?
   - Did the decision turn out to be right, and if not, how did you handle that?

4. **How have you grown as a leader over the past few years?**
   - Is there a specific, honest example of a leadership mistake and the change that followed?
   - Is the growth concrete, or just a vague claim like "I communicate better now"?

5. **Tell me about a time you had to lead through a major organizational change (reorg, pivot, layoffs affecting your team).**
   - Did you help your team stay productive and grounded during uncertainty?
   - Did you manage up (keeping leadership informed) and down (keeping the team informed) at the same time?

6. **Describe a situation where you had to balance short-term delivery pressure with long-term technical health.**
   - Did you make the tradeoff explicit to stakeholders, rather than silently sacrificing one for the other?
   - Was there a concrete plan to address the long-term side later, and did you follow through on it?

### Theme: Conflict (6 questions)

7. **Tell me about a time you disagreed with a peer on a technical decision.**
   - Did you engage with their reasoning, or just repeat your own opinion louder?
   - How was the disagreement resolved - data, escalation, compromise?

8. **Describe a conflict you had with a cross-functional partner (product, design, data science).**
   - Did you understand their perspective and constraints, not just push your own?
   - Did the relationship recover and improve afterward?

9. **Tell me about a time you had to deliver critical feedback to someone.**
   - Was the feedback specific and actionable, not vague or purely emotional?
   - Did you deliver it privately and respectfully?

10. **Tell me about a time two people or teams you worked with were in conflict, and you had to help resolve it.**
    - Did you stay neutral and focus on the shared goal?
    - Did you actually resolve the root cause, or just paper over the symptom?

11. **Describe a time you had to push back on your manager's decision.**
    - Did you raise the concern respectfully and with evidence?
    - Did you commit fully once the final decision was made, even if it wasn't yours?

12. **Tell me about a time you received harsh or unfair criticism. How did you respond?**
    - Did you react defensively, or extract any useful signal even from unfair feedback?
    - Did you address it directly with the person, rather than letting resentment build?

### Theme: Failure (6 questions)

13. **Tell me about your biggest professional failure.**
    - Is the failure real and significant, not a disguised success story?
    - Is there a clear, specific lesson, and evidence you applied it later?

14. **Describe a time a project you worked on got cancelled or deprioritized.**
    - How did you handle the disappointment and keep the team motivated?
    - Did you extract reusable learning or components from the cancelled work?

15. **Tell me about a time you made a technical decision that you later regretted.**
    - Do you own the decision honestly, without blaming others for your call?
    - Did you fix it yourself, or did someone else have to clean it up?

16. **Describe a time you missed a deadline. What happened?**
    - Did you communicate the slip early, or let it be a surprise?
    - What changed in how you estimate or plan afterward?

17. **Tell me about a time you shipped a bug that caused a significant customer or business impact.**
    - Did you take ownership immediately, or deflect to process/testing gaps only?
    - What specific guardrail did you add afterward (tests, monitoring, review process)?

18. **Tell me about a time your assumptions about a system or user turned out to be wrong.**
    - Did you validate assumptions with real data before building?
    - How did you adjust once you learned you were wrong?

### Theme: Ambiguity (6 questions)

19. **Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without complete information.**
    - Did you gather what reasonable information you could first, or just guess?
    - Was there a way to de-risk the decision (small test, phased rollout) rather than going all-in blind?

20. **Describe a project where the requirements kept changing.**
    - Did you build in a way that tolerated change, or rebuild from scratch each time?
    - Did you push back on scope churn when it was unreasonable?

21. **Tell me about a time you had to define your own scope because nobody gave you clear direction.**
    - Did you proactively seek clarity from stakeholders, or just freeze?
    - Did the scope you defined turn out to be the right one in hindsight?

22. **Describe a time you had to operate in a completely new domain or technology you weren't familiar with.**
    - How did you ramp up quickly - specific learning strategy, not just "I read docs"?
    - Did you still deliver meaningful value despite the unfamiliarity?

23. **Tell me about a time you had to prioritize between multiple urgent, conflicting demands.**
    - What framework or reasoning did you use to decide priority?
    - Did you communicate the tradeoff to the people whose requests got deprioritized?

24. **Describe a time the "right" technical answer wasn't clear, and reasonable engineers disagreed.**
    - Did you seek diverse input before deciding?
    - Did you make a timely decision anyway, rather than stalling indefinitely for certainty?

### Theme: Cross-team influence (6 questions)

25. **Tell me about a time you had to convince another team to change their roadmap to help you.**
    - Did you understand and address their incentives, not just your own need?
    - Did the collaboration actually happen, and was it sustained?

26. **Describe a time you built a tool or process that multiple teams adopted.**
    - Did other teams adopt it voluntarily because it was genuinely useful, or was it mandated?
    - What was the measurable impact across teams?

27. **Tell me about a time you had to align stakeholders with very different priorities.**
    - Did you find genuine common ground, or just compromise everyone into a worse outcome?
    - How did you keep the alignment from falling apart after the meeting ended?

28. **Describe a situation where you had no formal authority but needed broad organizational buy-in.**
    - What specific tactics did you use (data, pilot programs, influential allies)?
    - Did you measure and communicate the eventual impact to sustain support?

29. **Tell me about a time you represented your team's interests in a broader company-wide decision.**
    - Did you advocate effectively while still being a good partner to the rest of the company?
    - Was there a concrete outcome that benefited your team as a result?

30. **Describe a time you had to say no to a request from another team.**
    - Was the "no" clear and well-reasoned, with an alternative offered if possible?
    - Did the relationship with that team stay healthy afterward?

### Theme: Mentorship (5 questions)

31. **Tell me about a time you helped someone get promoted.**
    - Did you give them specific opportunities to demonstrate the next level's skills?
    - Was your support consistent over time, not a one-off conversation?

32. **Describe a time you had to manage out or give very tough feedback to someone struggling.**
    - Was the feedback timely, honest, and specific, not delayed or softened to the point of being useless?
    - Did you treat the person with respect even in a hard conversation?

33. **Tell me about a time you raised the bar for code quality or engineering practices on your team.**
    - Did you lead by example, or just mandate a new rule?
    - Did the change actually stick after you stopped actively pushing it?

34. **Describe your approach to onboarding new engineers onto your team.**
    - Is there a concrete process or practice you personally built or improved?
    - Can you point to a specific engineer whose ramp-up improved because of it?

35. **Tell me about someone you consider a mentee. How did that relationship develop?**
    - Is the relationship ongoing and reciprocal, or a one-time interaction being overstated?
    - What specific growth did you help enable?

### Theme: Delivery (5 questions)

36. **Tell me about the most complex project you've delivered.**
    - Is the complexity real (multiple systems, teams, or constraints), not just "a lot of code"?
    - Is your specific contribution clear within a larger team effort?

37. **Describe a time you had to deliver results despite a major unexpected obstacle.**
    - Did you adapt the plan quickly, or stick rigidly to the original plan past the point it made sense?
    - Was the final result actually delivered, or just "we tried hard"?

38. **Tell me about a time you had to cut scope to hit a deadline.**
    - Did you cut the right things (based on actual priority), not just the easiest things to cut?
    - Did you communicate the cut scope clearly to stakeholders rather than hiding it?

39. **Describe how you track and ensure quality on a large project with multiple contributors.**
    - Is there a specific, repeatable practice (review process, testing strategy, checklists)?
    - Did it actually catch real issues before they shipped?

40. **Tell me about a time you had to say "this isn't ready" and delay a launch.**
    - Was the call backed by specific evidence of risk, not just gut feeling or excessive caution?
    - Did you communicate the delay decision clearly and did leadership support it after seeing your reasoning?

## Part 2: Staff vs. senior signal differences

Many engineers prepare the same way for a senior loop and a staff loop, and this is a mistake. Interviewers at both levels ask similar-sounding questions, but they are listening for different scope and depth in the answer.

| Dimension | What a strong Senior answer shows | What a strong Staff answer shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope of impact** | Impact on your team or one immediate product area | Impact across multiple teams, a whole product line, or the broader organization |
| **Decision-making** | Makes sound decisions within a defined project | Defines the problem and the project itself, often before anyone else notices it needs solving |
| **Technical direction** | Executes and improves on a given technical approach | Sets technical direction that other senior engineers and teams align to |
| **Time horizon** | Thinks in terms of the current project or quarter | Thinks in terms of multiple quarters or years, anticipating future needs |
| **Influence** | Influences peers and immediate stakeholders | Influences across org boundaries, including people several levels senior or in entirely different departments |
| **Mentorship** | Mentors individual engineers directly | Multiplies impact by improving processes, raising the bar organization-wide, and growing other senior engineers |
| **Ambiguity handling** | Operates well when given a loosely defined problem | Identifies and frames problems that nobody has defined yet |
| **Failure stories** | Shows personal growth and recovery from a mistake | Shows systemic thinking - fixing not just the bug but the process or architecture that allowed the bug class to exist |

A simple self-check: if your stories all involve "I did X for my team," you are demonstrating senior-level scope. If at least a few involve "I noticed X was a problem across teams nobody had officially assigned, and I drove the fix across those teams," that's staff-level scope. If you are interviewing for staff but your story bank only has senior-scoped stories, go back to file 01 and look again at your last 10+ years - there is almost always at least one broader-scope moment you're underselling.

## Part 3: Negotiating your senior-level offer

Passing the interview loop is only half the job. For senior and staff roles, the gap between an unnegotiated offer and a well-negotiated one is often tens of thousands of dollars per year, sometimes more. This section covers the practical mechanics.

### Understand the total compensation structure first

Senior and staff offers at big tech companies are almost never just a salary number. Before you negotiate anything, make sure you understand every component you're being offered.

| Component | What it is | What to pay attention to |
|---|---|---|
| **Base salary** | Fixed annual pay, paid every paycheck | Usually the least negotiable piece at senior level relative to the others, but still matters, especially for loan/mortgage qualification |
| **Annual bonus / cash bonus target** | A percentage of base, often tied to company and individual performance | Ask whether it's "target" (expected typical payout) or "max" (rarely hit) |
| **Equity (RSUs or stock options)** | Shares or stock grants, usually vesting over 4 years | Check the vesting schedule (equal over 4 years vs. back-loaded), and whether it's RSUs (less risky) or options (riskier, tied to stock price growth) |
| **Sign-on bonus** | A one-time cash payment, often used to smooth out a gap from a previous employer's unvested equity or to bridge a lower first-year equity vest | Often the most flexible lever in a negotiation since it's a one-time cost to the company, not a recurring one |
| **Refresh grants** | Additional equity granted in future years, separate from the initial grant | Ask how refresh grants are typically decided (performance-based, standard for the level, etc.) |
| **Level** | Your job level (e.g., L5/L6 at various companies, or "Senior"/"Staff" titles) | This determines your salary band, equity band, and future promotion path - often more impactful long-term than any single number |
| **Benefits** | Health insurance, retirement matching, parental leave, etc. | Usually standardized and non-negotiable, but worth confirming for a complete comparison between offers |

### Why "total comp" framing matters

When companies and recruiters talk about an offer, they almost always mean **total compensation**: base + average annual bonus + average annual equity value (grant divided by vesting years) + any sign-on averaged over the vesting period. Always convert every offer to this same total comp shape before comparing, because a company with a lower base but a much larger equity grant might have a higher total comp than one with a flashier base salary.

### Using levels.fyi and similar data sources

Sites like levels.fyi aggregate self-reported offers by company, level, and location, and are one of the most useful free tools for senior/staff negotiation. A few practical tips:

- Filter by your exact level, not just the company, since compensation bands differ enormously between levels.
- Filter by location if possible - total comp for the same level can differ significantly between cities and countries.
- Treat the numbers as a reference range, not a guaranteed number. Self-reported data can skew toward people who negotiated well or who are proud of a high number.
- Cross-check with multiple sources (Blind, Glassdoor, your own network) rather than relying on a single site.

### The role of competing offers

The single most effective negotiation lever at senior and staff level is a genuine competing offer from another comparable company. Recruiters and hiring managers have real flexibility to match or beat a competing offer, especially for senior/staff roles where losing a strong candidate is costly to replace.

A few important notes on using competing offers honestly and effectively:

- **Be truthful.** Never invent or exaggerate a competing offer. Companies sometimes verify offer details, and more importantly, misrepresenting facts in a negotiation is a serious trust violation that can follow you if discovered.
- **Timing matters.** Try to get your offers to land within a similar window so you can negotiate them against each other while both are still live, rather than one offer expiring before the next one even arrives.
- **Share the right amount of detail.** You don't need to reveal every number from a competing offer. It's enough to say "I have a competing offer with a total comp around $X" and let the recruiter decide how to respond.

### Negotiation scripts: example phrasing

Below are example scripts for common negotiation moments. Adjust specific numbers and company names to your real situation - these are templates for tone and structure, not word-for-word scripts to copy blindly.

**Opening the negotiation after receiving an offer:**

> "Thank you so much for the offer, I'm genuinely excited about the team and the role. Before I give you a final answer, I'd like to take a few days to review everything carefully, and I'd also like to talk through the compensation package once I've had time to think it over."

**Using a competing offer:**

> "I want to be upfront with you. I have another offer in hand with a total compensation around [$X], and I'd genuinely prefer to join your team if we can get closer to that number. Is there flexibility on the equity or sign-on to help bridge that gap?"

**Asking for a level review, not just more money:**

> "Based on the scope of the work described for this role and some of the leveling guides I've looked at, I think my experience might map better to [level above the one offered]. Would it be possible to revisit the leveling before we finalize the offer?"

**Negotiating sign-on bonus specifically (useful for bridging unvested equity from a current employer):**

> "I currently have about [$X] in unvested equity that I'd be walking away from. Would it be possible to structure a sign-on bonus to help offset that gap, especially since it wouldn't be a recurring cost on your end?"

**Responding to "this is our best and final offer":**

> "I appreciate you sharing that, and I understand there are constraints on your end. Can you help me understand which part of the package has the most flexibility, even if the total number is fixed - for example, the mix between sign-on and equity?"

**Closing the negotiation gracefully, whether accepting or not:**

> "Thank you for working with me on this. Based on where we've landed, I'm happy to move forward and accept the offer."
> or
> "I really appreciate the time and effort everyone put into this process. After careful consideration, I've decided to go a different direction, but I'd love to stay in touch for future opportunities."

### A few more practical negotiation tips for senior/staff candidates

- **Never give the first number if you can avoid it.** If a recruiter asks for your salary expectations early, it's reasonable to say "I'd like to learn more about the role and the level first before discussing specific numbers."
- **Negotiate once you have the full written offer, not verbally mid-process.** Written offers are easier to compare and harder to walk back once agreed to verbally without full information.
- **Everything is more negotiable than it looks.** Base salary tends to be the least flexible; sign-on bonus and equity tend to be the most flexible, since they are easier for a company to adjust without disrupting internal pay equity across existing employees.
- **Get everything in writing before resigning from your current job.** Verbal promises during a negotiation call are not binding; wait for the updated written offer letter.
- **Consider the whole package, not just the biggest number.** A slightly lower total comp at a company with a much better team, growth trajectory, or work-life balance can be the better long-term decision. Compensation is important, but it is one input among several.

## Wrapping up this track

You now have a complete behavioral and leadership interview preparation path: a story bank built with the STARL method (file 01), a clear map of what each major company is actually scoring you on (file 02), and a full question bank plus a practical negotiation playbook (this file).

What stands out, and what actually gets you the offer at the level you deserve, is a true story told clearly, with your own specific judgment and actions at the center of it.
